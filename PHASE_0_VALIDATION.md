# CreatorWorks Phase 0 Validation

## Purpose

Phase 0 tests two things together:

1. Whether CreatorWorks helps a creator move from comparison and self-doubt toward an appropriate first invitation.
2. Whether sharing works as a **complete handoff**—not merely sending a link—so a relevant person can understand, reach, and experience the work, then return with useful feedback, without the creator standing beside them.

The larger question is whether the complete exchange creates more value than sending someone a software link and a generic survey.

No production application, accounts, public discovery, payments, or marketplace features should be built during this phase.

## Central Question

> Can CreatorWorks help a creator decide that their work is ready for one honest conversation—and then hand that work to one relevant person so well that the person can understand it, experience it, and respond usefully on their own?

The first half is about **courage**: is the work ready for a small, honest invitation? The second half is about **the handoff**: once shared, can the work make a good first impression and complete the loop without the creator explaining every step?

## The Manual CreatorWorks Experience Session

Phase 0 centers on one thing: a **manual CreatorWorks Experience Session**. There is no application code, connection kit, accounts, payments, hosting, or public discovery. A facilitator stands in for everything CreatorWorks will later do on its own.

For Phase 0, the experience mode is a **guided external link**: CreatorWorks explains what to do, the participant reaches the work through an ordinary link, and the return to feedback is manual. (The connected, embedded, and managed-hosting modes described in the product plan are not part of Phase 0.)

A manual session moves through the same states the product plan defines:

1. Invitation opened
2. Experience understood
3. Experience started
4. Meaningful point reached
5. Participant returned
6. Response received
7. Creator acknowledged
8. Outcome shared

These states describe where the relationship stands, not what the participant does inside the work. Phase 0 does not track clicks, keystrokes, uploaded files, or private content. Automated activity could show that something happened; only the participant's feedback shows whether it mattered.

## Sharing as a Complete Handoff

Sending a link is not the same as sharing. Sharing has succeeded only when the person on the other side can carry the whole experience themselves.

For each session, test whether a relevant recipient can, on their own:

- Understand what the work is.
- Understand why it may matter to them.
- Know who made it.
- Recognize whether it is early, experimental, or mature.
- Reach and experience the work.
- Follow any necessary installation or access instructions.
- Understand what the creator wants them to notice.
- Return to CreatorWorks with useful feedback.
- Complete this experience without the creator standing beside them or explaining every step.

The sharing loop being tested is:

> **Present → Share → Experience → Respond → Recognize**

- **Present** — The creator explains the work honestly and clearly.
- **Share** — The work is placed in someone else's hands with a warm, relevant introduction.
- **Experience** — The recipient reaches and understands the work with little friction.
- **Respond** — The recipient returns with useful feedback.
- **Recognize** — Both people see that the contribution mattered.

The point of Phase 0 is to find exactly where this handoff succeeds and where it breaks.

## Courage-Through-Evidence Hypothesis

Creators do not need broad reassurance that everything they make is good enough to publish. They need a credible standard that separates a small learning invitation from a finished public launch.

The Phase 0 hypothesis is:

> When a creator can confirm that something real is experienceable, its state is honest, its intended person is clear, one meaningful question can be asked, and they are willing to listen, sharing with one relevant person will feel responsible and achievable rather than exposing or premature.

The prototype should reduce unproductive comparison without lowering quality, honesty, privacy, or safety standards.

## What We Are Testing

The Phase 0 prototype covers readiness, a complete sharing handoff, and one complete loop:

1. A creator checks whether the work is ready for one honest conversation.
2. The creator presents testable software honestly.
3. The work is shared so a relevant person understands it and why they were invited—without a live explanation.
4. The person recognizes the work's current state (early, experimental, or mature) and any disclosures.
5. The person reaches and experiences the work, following any access or installation guidance on their own.
6. The person understands what the creator wants them to notice.
7. The person returns and submits a short, structured response.
8. The creator acknowledges it.
9. The creator records what was learned, decided, or changed.
10. The person sees the outcome and knows their time mattered.

## What We Are Not Testing

- Public discovery
- Marketplace demand
- Payments or pricing
- Social feeds or follower systems
- Code hosting or software deployment
- Automated matching
- Large-scale moderation
- Visual popularity
- The number of link clicks, page views, or impressions

## Prototype Contents

The prototype includes:

- A CreatorWorks homepage
- A creator-readiness experience
- A realistic project page for fictional software
- A personal invitation written in the creator's voice
- A simple, memorable share link
- A QR-code example for sharing in person
- A message or email preview the creator could send
- A trust summary shown before entering the work
- A clear **Try it** or **Get it** action
- Access or installation guidance for reaching the work
- A clear, manual route back from the work to the feedback experience
- A short feedback experience
- A creator acknowledgment and outcome update

The fictional interface example is **SetList Flow**, a prototype that helps performing musicians organize songs, notes, and stage cues in one place. It exists only to show what a project page and invitation look like, so people can react to the CreatorWorks interface without prior knowledge.

SetList Flow is not a real session. The first real Phase 0 session uses Codex Studio (see [CODEX_STUDIO_VALIDATION.md](CODEX_STUDIO_VALIDATION.md)), and the two must be kept clearly distinct. Using a fictional example keeps the interface demonstration separate from existing projects. The share link, QR code, trust summary, and message preview can all be mocked up on paper or as simple images; nothing here requires building working software.

## Founding Validation Group

Recruit:

- Five independent software creators with testable prototypes or early releases
- At least three relevant participants for each creator
- A mix of technical experience, audience size, and professional background

These are learning targets, not growth targets.

## Creator Session

### Before the session

Ask the creator to bring:

- A working prototype or early release
- A one-sentence description of whom it helps
- One specific question they need answered
- A safe link or demonstration another person can experience

### Session tasks

Without coaching, ask the creator to:

1. Read the homepage.
2. Explain what they believe CreatorWorks does.
3. Describe how they currently decide whether work is "good enough" to share.
4. Complete the readiness experience for their own work.
5. Explain whether the result changed how sharing felt and why.
6. Review the example project page.
7. Draft the essential information for their own project page.
8. Choose one feedback goal.
9. Decide on one appropriate person to invite.
10. Draft a personal invitation in their own voice and choose how they would send it (link, QR code, or message).
11. Review an example participant response.
12. Write an acknowledgment and outcome update.

### Creator interview questions

- What did you expect CreatorWorks to do after the first screen?
- Before the readiness check, what made you hesitate to share your work?
- Did "ready for one honest conversation" feel different from "ready to publish"? How?
- Did any readiness statement feel like empty encouragement or lower the standard too far?
- Did the readiness result make you more willing to invite one appropriate person?
- Did the experience help you stop comparing your work with unrelated polished products?
- Did the invitation sound like you, or did it feel like marketing?
- Would you feel comfortable sending this invitation without also explaining the work in person?
- Which part felt immediately useful?
- Which part felt like extra work?
- What would prevent you from presenting a project here?
- Did the feedback request help you ask a better question?
- Did the acknowledgment and outcome step improve the exchange?
- What information would you be unwilling to publish?
- What would make you trust CreatorWorks with your work and relationships?
- Would you return for another feedback round? Why or why not?

## Participant Session

The participant session is the real test of the handoff. Wherever possible, let the person go through the experience **as if the creator were not there.** Only step in if they become truly stuck, and record where that happened.

### Before the session

Tell the participant:

- The software may be unfinished.
- CreatorWorks is testing the invitation and feedback experience, not judging the participant.
- They may stop at any time.
- Their prototype answers will not be published without permission.

### Session tasks

Without coaching, ask the participant to:

1. Open the invitation as they received it (link, QR code, or message).
2. Explain what the software is, who made it, and why they were invited.
3. Say whether the work seems early, experimental, or mature.
4. Find the work's current state and important disclosures.
5. Explain what they expect before following the product link.
6. Reach the work and follow any access or installation guidance on their own.
7. Review or simulate trying the example, and say what the creator wanted them to notice.
8. Find their way back to the feedback experience.
9. Complete the structured response.
10. Review the acknowledgment and outcome update.
11. Explain what they believe happened because they participated.

### Participant interview questions

- Did you understand what you were being asked to do without someone explaining it?
- From the invitation alone, did you understand what the work is and why it might matter to you?
- Could you tell who made it and whether it was early or finished?
- Did you know what the creator wanted you to notice?
- Was it clear how to reach the work, and did any access or installation step confuse you?
- After trying it, did you know how to get back and respond?
- Did the project page respect your time?
- Were the work's state, limitations, and external-link expectations clear?
- Did the feedback questions help you respond thoughtfully?
- Did any question ask for too much effort or information?
- Did you feel pressured to be positive?
- Was the creator's acknowledgment meaningful?
- Could you tell what was learned, decided, or changed?
- Did you want public recognition, private acknowledgment, or neither?
- Would you participate again? Why or why not?

## Observation Guide

Record behavior before asking for opinions.

Watch for:

- Whether the purpose is understood without explanation
- Words that sound like software development, research, or business language rather than a person sharing something they made
- Labels that require explanation before someone knows where they lead
- Places where warmer wording becomes vague or less trustworthy
- Confusion between CreatorWorks and a software-building service
- Confusion between readiness for one invitation and readiness for a public launch
- Reassurance that feels flattering but unsupported
- A readiness check that encourages unsafe, misleading, or untestable work
- Continued fixation on whether other creators are more talented
- Whether the creator can name one appropriate person and one useful question
- Whether the invitation sounds like the creator or like advertising
- Confusion between the project page and the externally hosted software
- Uncertainty about why the participant was invited
- Missed disclosures or unclear expectations
- Whether the recipient can reach the work without help
- Where an access step, download, or installation instruction causes friction
- Whether the recipient understands what to notice before trying
- Whether the recipient can find the way back to feedback
- Feedback prompts that produce vague answers
- Pressure to praise the creator or accept public credit
- Friction caused by identity or privacy choices
- Confusion between acknowledgment and agreement
- Whether the outcome feels more meaningful than a generic thank-you
- Any dead end without a clear next step
- Every moment the creator had to step in to keep the handoff moving

### Where the handoff breaks

For each session, note where the experience succeeded and where it broke along this path:

> **Invitation → Understanding → Access → Experience → Response**

- **Invitation** — Did the person open it and feel invited rather than sold to?
- **Understanding** — Did they understand what the work is, who made it, its state, and why it may help them?
- **Access** — Could they reach and, if needed, install the work on their own?
- **Experience** — Could they try it and notice what the creator hoped they would?
- **Response** — Did they return and give useful feedback?

The most valuable Phase 0 result is a clear picture of which link in this chain fails, and why.

## Session Scorecard

For each session, mark each statement as **Yes**, **Partly**, or **No** and record the evidence.

### Comprehension

- The person understood CreatorWorks without coaching.
- The visible wording felt familiar, warm, and trustworthy.
- The step names described recognizable human actions without technical explanation.
- The person understood what exists in version one.
- The person did not expect CreatorWorks to build, host, discover customers for, or sell the software.
- The creator understood that readiness means ready for one honest conversation, not proof of quality.

### Creator courage

- The readiness standard felt credible rather than flattering.
- The creator could identify something real another person could experience.
- The creator could name one relevant person and one meaningful question.
- Comparison with more experienced creators became less important to the next decision.
- A small invitation felt achievable without creating pressure to launch publicly.

### Sharing handoff

- From the invitation alone, the person understood what the work is and why it might matter.
- The person could tell who made the work and whether it was early, experimental, or mature.
- The person understood what the creator wanted them to notice.
- The person reached the work and completed any access or installation step on their own.
- The person found the way back to the feedback experience without help.
- The person completed the whole experience without the creator standing beside them.

### Task clarity

- The creator knew what information to present.
- The participant knew what to experience.
- The participant knew what kind of response would help.
- Both understood what would happen next.

### Trust and dignity

- The work's state and limitations were clear.
- The external handoff felt safe and understandable.
- Privacy and recognition choices were clear.
- Neither person felt manipulated, exploited, or pressured.

### Human value

- The creator gained useful learning.
- The participant believed their time mattered.
- The acknowledgment felt genuine.
- The recorded outcome made the exchange feel complete.
- Both would consider repeating the experience.

## Success Measures for the Handoff

Judge the handoff by whether the right person could complete the intended experience—**not** by how many links, clicks, page views, or impressions it produced.

For each session, record success or breakage at each step of:

> **Invitation → Understanding → Access → Experience → Response**

Useful measures include:

- Whether the person understood the work and why it mattered from the invitation alone.
- Whether the person could tell who made it and what state it was in.
- Whether the person reached and experienced the work without avoidable help.
- Whether any access or installation step caused confusion, and where.
- Whether the person understood what to notice.
- Whether the person found the way back and left useful feedback.
- How many times, if any, the creator had to step in.
- Whether the whole experience felt clear, fair, respectful, and complete to both people.

Do not treat link clicks, page views, or impressions as primary success measures. A single completed handoff that produced a thoughtful response is worth more than many opened links.

## Completed-Loop Record

For each work, record:

- Work and creator
- Intended participant
- Feedback goal
- How the work was shared (link, QR code, or message)
- Date invited
- Whether the person understood the work from the invitation alone
- Whether the person reached and experienced the work without help
- Where the handoff broke, if it did
- Whether the work was meaningfully experienced
- Response received
- Creator acknowledgment
- Learning, decision, or change recorded
- Participant notified
- Participant reaction to the outcome
- Problems, recovery, and unresolved risks

## Initial Learning Threshold

Before recommending a private founding beta, complete:

- Five creator sessions
- At least fifteen participant invitations
- At least five complete loops across three or more different works

Move forward only when:

- People consistently understand the purpose without extensive explanation.
- Creators consistently distinguish a small invitation from a public launch.
- The readiness experience reduces unproductive self-doubt without declaring every work ready.
- Creators can name one relevant person and one meaningful question.
- A relevant person can understand, reach, and experience a work and return with feedback without the creator guiding every step.
- Creators receive useful learning rather than generic praise.
- Participants consider their time worthwhile.
- Acknowledgment and outcome feel meaningfully better than a survey thank-you.
- Repeated confusion has a clear correction.
- No unresolved trust, consent, ownership, privacy, or safety problem undermines the experience.

## Decision After Phase 0

Choose one outcome:

- **Proceed** — The loop creates clear value and is ready for a private founding beta.
- **Revise and retest** — The idea is promising, but important confusion or friction remains.
- **Narrow the audience** — The loop works for a more specific creator or participant than expected.
- **Stop** — The complete exchange is not meaningfully better than existing simple tools.

Do not proceed merely because the prototype looks polished or participants say they like the idea.
